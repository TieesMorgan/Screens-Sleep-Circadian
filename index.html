01 · Learning destination

What will learners take away?

Core learning objectives

  1. Explain how evening light affects melatonin, circadian timing, sleep, and next-day functioning.
  2. Connect these effects to developmental, psychological, and social conditions in emerging adulthood.
  3. Compare five digital wellness strategies and appraise their evidence and limitations.
  4. Adapt choices to family, work, cultural, and accessibility needs.
  5. Practise selecting and evaluating a realistic digital wellness action.

One central message

The goal is not simply “less screen time.” It is better-timed light, realistic boundaries, supportive routines, and stronger self-regulation skills.

The five strategies form a flexible continuum—not a single prescription.

This module introduces and supports skills; it is not itself a tested clinical intervention.

Audio overview transcript (about one minute)

Evening technology use can affect both when we fall asleep and how well we rest. For emerging adults, this matters because school, work, friendships and changing routines often collide at bedtime. This module explains how light reaches the biological clock, why social and emotional pressures can keep us connected late at night, and how to evaluate five strategies: spectral display tuning, a bedroom sanctuary, a disconnection window, chronotherapy, and social and emotional learning skills. These are not equally strong or suitable for everyone. We distinguish laboratory evidence from associations and preliminary interventions, and we consider work schedules, housing, caregiving and cultural realities. By the end, you will be able to choose and critique a practical digital wellness approach rather than simply follow a screen-time rule.

02 · Population

Why emerging adults?

Emerging adulthood is often described as the late teens through the twenties: a transition in which people take increasing responsibility for schedules, relationships, work and wellbeing (Arnett, 2007; Roisman et al., 2004).

Autonomy

Less external structure

Family routines and school rules often give way to self-managed bedtimes, deadlines and devices.

Connection

Peer responsiveness

FOMO, relationship maintenance and expectations of instant replies can prolong bedtime media use (Vaterlaus et al., 2016).

Adjustment

Emotional demands

New roles, financial pressures and academic uncertainty can heighten stress and pre-sleep arousal (Conley, 2015).

Important developmental qualification

Arnett's framework is not universal. Caregiving, immigration, socioeconomic circumstances, culture and employment can change what adulthood looks like. These differences also change which sleep strategies are feasible (Katsiaficas et al., 2015; Tsipianitis et al., 2019).

03 · Research foundation

The problem, purpose, and potential value

The research problem

Evening light-emitting devices may delay the circadian clock and alter sleep. Interactive media can also displace bedtime and increase cognitive or emotional arousal. Poor sleep is associated with difficulties in mood, attention and daytime functioning (Chang et al., 2015; Bhat et al., 2018; Green et al., 2017).

The module's purpose

To evaluate five research-informed approaches that address biological light exposure, behavioural boundaries, delayed sleep timing and emotional self-regulation in emerging adults.

The intended impact

Support more informed and achievable decisions about evening technology use, while recognizing that improving sleep and mental health requires more than a single device setting or online lesson.

Aims are not demonstrated outcomes: this learning module has not yet been evaluated for improvements in sleep, mood, or long-term behaviour.

04 · Understand the mechanism

From screen light to the body clock

Light is a timing signal. The effect of an evening display depends on spectrum, brightness, duration, timing and a person's light history—not simply whether a screen looks blue.

Light-driven effects

  • Melatonin: may be suppressed during evening exposure under specific laboratory conditions.
  • Circadian timing: some controlled experiments show a delayed biological night.
  • Sleep: timing and some sleep measures may change, depending on the exposure.

Behaviour-driven effects

  • Sleep displacement: time online can replace time allotted to sleep.
  • Pre-sleep arousal: stressful study, gaming or messaging can keep attention activated.
  • Interruptions: overnight alerts can disturb a sleep opportunity.
Explore the research: selected experimental findings and their limits

Chang et al. (2015) compared multi-evening eReader exposure with a print-book condition and found changes in melatonin, circadian timing and morning alertness. Schöllhorn et al. (2023) investigated how evening display melanopic irradiance affects biological outcomes. These controlled studies establish plausible light-related mechanisms under the tested conditions, but their findings cannot be turned into a universal percentage for every phone, room, or user.

Interpretation: laboratory exposure conditions, sample sizes and outcome measurements differ. Delayed phase is not the same thing as measured clinical insomnia.

Explore the research: sleep architecture, cognition, and measurement

Green et al. (2017) and Höhn et al. (2024) examine sleep-related outcomes under particular light and device conditions; effects may vary across developmental groups. DLMO (dim-light melatonin onset) is a marker of internal circadian timing, while actigraphy and polysomnography assess different aspects of sleep. Self-reported sleepiness and cognitive tests measure other outcomes—not interchangeable measures of “good sleep.”

Potential moderators include chronotype, previous daytime light exposure, individual light sensitivity, media content and prior sleep loss (Van der Maren et al., 2018; Hartstein et al., 2024).

05 · Digital wellness strategies

Five strategies, matched to real needs

The continuum moves from lower-burden harm reduction to more demanding approaches for delayed sleep timing, with social-emotional skill development supporting all tiers. These are flexible choices, not a ranking of effectiveness.

Why these five belong together

Strategies 1–2: adaptations that can preserve necessary evening connectivity. Strategies 3–4: deeper changes when feasible or appropriate. Strategy 5: self-awareness and self-management skills that support any approach.

1 · Spectral display tuning — reduce biologically active evening light
Harm reductionExperimental mechanism

What: modify the timing, amount or spectral properties of evening device light where continued screen use is necessary; use device settings as a practical option while recognizing their limitations.

Need addressed: emerging adults may need screens for assignments, work and essential relationships rather than being able to switch off completely.

Practical example: dim unnecessary room and display light, activate scheduled night settings for comfort, and avoid treating an amber tint as proof that circadian effects have been eliminated.

Evidence: Schöllhorn et al. (2023) studied melanopic display light and laboratory outcomes. The evidence directly supports the importance of spectral exposure; it does not certify every consumer filter or pair of glasses.

Barriers/moderators: colour-critical work, uncalibrated filters, duration, prior daylight, eye-level brightness and individual sensitivity.

What to assess: feasible light changes, sleep timing and subjective alertness; specialized studies may measure melanopic illuminance and DLMO.

2 · Bedroom sanctuary — separate sleep from interactive media
Harm reductionObservational association

What: move notifications and interactive content away from the sleep period; a phone can remain nearby for essential responsibilities but be placed out of reach or configured to admit priority contacts.

Need addressed: FOMO, peer pressure for immediate replies, bedtime scrolling and anxiety-driven checking.

Practical example: set a priority-contact exception, keep the phone off the pillow and agree on a reply boundary with a close friend.

Evidence: Bhat et al. (2018) reported an association between in-bed social media use and insomnia in a cross-sectional sample of 855 adults (mean age 43.6), so causation and direct generalization to emerging adults are not established.

Barriers/moderators: phone-as-alarm, family caregiving, roommates, housing constraints and baseline anxiety.

What to assess: frequency of in-bed checking, perceived sleep quality and perceived anxiety; changes should not be attributed solely to the phone rule.

3 · Disconnection window — protect a wind-down period
Adaptable wind-downMechanistic + behavioural rationale

What: experiment with a lower-stimulation window before sleep; the source capstone proposes 60–90 minutes, but learners can adapt the duration to circumstances.

Need addressed: less structured routines, bedtime procrastination, stress and late-night cognitive activation.

Practical example: start with 20–30 minutes of journaling, stretching or print reading on nights when school or work makes longer disconnection unrealistic, then reassess.

Evidence: controlled studies such as Chinoy et al. (2018) and Green et al. (2017) support concerns about evening exposure and sleep-related outcomes, but do not establish one universally effective cutoff for all emerging adults.

Barriers/moderators: late shifts, deadlines, chronotype, task arousal and prior sleep debt.

What to assess: actual bedtime, sleep opportunity, perceived sleepiness and ability to maintain the routine.

4 · Chronotherapy — align daily light and sleep timing
More intensivePromising small studies

What: structured attention to morning light, consistent wake timing and evening light reduction; targeted chronotherapeutic plans should be individualized, particularly when sleep delay or mood symptoms are pronounced.

Need addressed: later circadian preference, social jetlag, difficulty meeting morning obligations and related mood concerns.

Practical example: discuss a consistent wake-time and morning-light plan with a qualified health professional when significant delayed timing persists.

Evidence: Wescott et al. (2026) compared a morning bright-light approach (N = 21) and a multicomponent approach (N = 15) in emerging adults. The latter showed promising circadian and mood findings, but the samples were small and further randomized research is needed.

Barriers/moderators: winter daylight, shift work, weekend schedules, adherence and clinical context. Safety: this module does not prescribe light-box intensity or timing; people with mood disorders, eye conditions or other health considerations should obtain individualized advice.

What to assess: sleep/wake regularity, social jetlag, mood and, in research settings, circadian phase markers.

5 · SEL skills training — build sustainable self-regulation
Supports every tierReview of diverse programs

What: practise noticing emotional triggers, managing stress, communicating boundaries and making decisions that fit everyday responsibilities.

Need addressed: identity transitions, peer belonging, academic stress, loneliness, anxiety, emotional coping and developing self-management.

Practical example: notice the urge to check messages, name the emotion behind it, select a coping action, then practise a considerate response-time boundary.

Evidence: Conley's (2015) Chapter 13 reviews 113 universal SEL-related prevention and promotion programs in higher education settings. Summarizing a related analysis, the chapter reports that supervised-practice programs were seven times more likely to produce significant outcomes than information-only programs and five times more likely than skills programs without supervised practice. These comparisons concern broad SEL outcomes, not demonstrated sleep effects of this webpage.

Barriers/moderators: stigma, workshop attendance, institutional support and need for actual guided practice. A self-paced module introduces the concepts but should not be described as equivalent to a supervised SEL intervention.

What to assess: practice logs, self-regulation reflections and, in formal programs, validated distress or SEL outcomes.

Return to the scenario

For the student working and caregiving late, a rigid 9 p.m. phone ban may be impractical. A priority-contact boundary or lower-light setup could be a feasible first step; a longer wind-down could be tried when shifts permit. The learner's own constraints and preferences determine what is realistic.

06 · Critical appraisal

How strong is the evidence?

“Evidence-informed” does not mean that all five strategies have equally strong, directly applicable support. The type of research matters as much as the headline finding.

Controlled experiments

Stronger for biological mechanisms

Light and screen experiments can help establish effects of a tested exposure on melatonin or circadian phase. Their controlled conditions do not necessarily represent everyday mixed lighting and varied device use.

Examples: Chang et al. (2015); Schöllhorn et al. (2023).

Cross-sectional studies

Useful for associations, not causal claims

In-bed media studies identify relationships with insomnia, mood or short sleep, but reverse causation and confounding remain plausible. An adult sample may not directly represent ages 18–29.

Example: Bhat et al. (2018), mean age 43.6 years.

Emerging-adult intervention studies

Promising but preliminary for chronotherapy

Wescott et al. (2026) reports two small studies (N = 21 and N = 15). A roughly 35-minute circadian phase advance was reported for the multicomponent approach, but larger controlled trials are needed before broad recommendations.

Review and transferable evidence

SEL strengthens the rationale for guided practice

Conley (2015, Chapter 13) reviews 113 universal prevention and promotion programs, spanning colleges, universities, trade/vocational schools and graduate/professional education. They target general social-emotional outcomes, not specifically bedtime screen behaviour.

What Conley (2015) actually supports

Seven times more likely to yield significant outcomes versus psychoeducation-only programs, and five times more likely versus skills programs without supervised practice (as reported in Chapter 13, citing Conley et al., 2013). These are comparisons of the likelihood of statistically significant results—not claims that students' sleep improves sevenfold or that lectures universally produce zero change.

Limitations: different interventions and outcomes, mostly short-term measurement, limited follow-up, and uncertain transfer to a self-guided screen-and-sleep module.

Research appraisal: where should we be cautious?
  • Population fit: some cited sleep-media studies use broader adult or sex-specific samples, not representative emerging-adult cohorts.
  • Mechanism versus intervention: demonstrating that a screen exposure changes melatonin does not prove that a consumer night filter prevents insomnia.
  • Associations versus causation: poor sleepers may use their devices more, and stress can affect both sleep and media use.
  • Preliminary trials: small samples and short follow-up limit how confidently results can be generalized.
  • Evaluation gap: the learning module itself has not been experimentally evaluated for behavioural or health outcomes.

For further evidence appraisal, inspect the linked primary studies in the reference list and consider sample, comparison group, effect size, outcome type and follow-up.

07 · Culture, context & feasibility

Digital wellness is not one-size-fits-all

A strategy that depends on complete evening disconnection may be inaccessible to someone who works nights, studies on a phone, shares a room or provides family care.

Shift work or evening classes

Barrier: bedtime varies or necessary work happens late.

Adaptation: focus on controllable light, notification and wind-down choices rather than one fixed clock time.

Shared or multigenerational housing

Barrier: limited private space and family responsibilities.

Adaptation: create a small sleep cue and prioritise essential contacts instead of removing every device from the room.

Access and affordability

Barrier: a smartphone may be the primary device for learning, work or communication.

Adaptation: try free settings, communication agreements and routine changes before considering equipment purchases.

Equity lens: ask learners what is feasible before asking them to change a habit. Arnett's developmental framework should be discussed alongside cultural and socioeconomic variation (Katsiaficas et al., 2015; Tsipianitis et al., 2019).

Retrieval and transfer

Scenario prompts, strategy comparison and a short knowledge check invite learners to apply ideas rather than only read them. Feedback can inform revision.

A quiz checks understanding; sustained behaviour change would need separate evaluation.

How could the module be evaluated in future?

Learning: compare brief pre/post questions about evidence types, mechanisms and realistic strategy selection. Usability: ask about clarity, navigation, accessibility barriers and relevance. Behaviour: optional follow-up logs can explore whether learners try a strategy, without assuming this online activity causes better sleep. More intensive research: validated sleep measures and controlled comparisons would be needed to test health-related outcomes.

The intended audiences include emerging adults, student-wellness programs and higher-education educators. In a classroom, an instructor can supply the guided practice and discussion that a standalone webpage does not provide.

Questions classmates may ask: suggested research-informed responses

Why not recommend eliminating screens?

Emerging adults often need technology for work, learning, caregiving and connection. A realistic continuum allows context-sensitive harm reduction when full disconnection is not possible.

Are all five strategies equally evidence-based?

No. The light mechanism has controlled experimental support; the in-bed-media evidence is largely correlational; chronotherapy findings are promising but preliminary; SEL research supports guided skills practice for broader social-emotional outcomes. The module itself has not been tested for long-term health effects.

Does the Conley review show that this website will improve sleep?

No. Chapter 13 summarizes 113 SEL-related programs in higher education. Its comparisons support the value of supervised practice for social-emotional outcomes, not a direct causal claim about this self-guided digital wellness module.

Do night mode or blue-light glasses completely solve the problem?

No. Light effects depend on the exposure, and behavioural factors such as alerting content, bedtime displacement and interruptions also matter. Commercial device modes vary and are not interchangeable with calibrated experimental displays.

Closing takeaway

Digital wellness for emerging adults is less about banning technology and more about making informed, evidence-sensitive and realistic choices about when, how and why we use it.
